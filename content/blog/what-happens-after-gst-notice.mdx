# What happens after you receive a GST notice — and the first three things to do

When you receive a GST notice, the tax department formally alerts you to discrepancies, missing returns, or tax shortfalls. The first three things you must do are: note the reply deadline (usually <Verify>15 to 30 days</Verify>), identify the specific section code (like <Verify>Section 73</Verify> or <Verify>Section 74</Verify>), and gather all relevant invoices, returns, and reconciliations to draft a fact-based response.

Receiving a communication from the Goods and Services Tax (GST) department can be a highly stressful event for any business owner, professional, or corporate entity. In the modern, technology-driven taxation landscape, data analytics and automated cross-matching of returns frequently trigger these notices. Whether it is a mismatch between your sales declared and the input tax credit claimed, a delay in filing returns, or an audit query, the tax authorities are constantly monitoring compliance. 

However, panic is not the solution. A notice is simply a request for information, clarification, or a demand for what the department perceives as unpaid dues. It provides you with an opportunity to present your case, submit your records, and resolve the discrepancy. In many instances, these notices are auto-generated due to minor data entry errors or timing differences in invoice uploads by your vendors. 

To handle a GST notice effectively, you need a systematic approach. Understanding what the notice entails, knowing your legal rights and obligations, and preparing a robust, evidence-backed reply can make the difference between a swift resolution and prolonged, costly litigation. This guide will walk you through the entire process, exploring the anatomy of GST notices, the immediate actions you must take, the various types of notices you might encounter, and the subsequent steps if matters escalate.

## What are the most common reasons for receiving a GST notice?

The GST network processes millions of transactions daily, and its sophisticated algorithms are designed to flag anomalies. Consequently, most notices stem from mismatches between different sets of data filed on the portal.

One of the most frequent triggers is a discrepancy between the outward supplies declared in your <Verify>GSTR-1</Verify> and the tax paid in your <Verify>GSTR-3B</Verify>. If the liability shown in <Verify>GSTR-1</Verify> exceeds the payment made in <Verify>GSTR-3B</Verify>, the system automatically detects a potential short payment of tax. The department may issue a notice under <Verify>Section 73</Verify> for normal cases or <Verify>Section 74</Verify> if they suspect fraud or intentional evasion.

Another common reason is an Input Tax Credit (ITC) mismatch. This occurs when the ITC you have claimed in your <Verify>GSTR-3B</Verify> does not align with the ITC available in your auto-generated <Verify>GSTR-2A</Verify> or <Verify>GSTR-2B</Verify>. The government has strictly tied ITC claims to vendor compliance. If your supplier has failed to upload the invoice or pay the tax, your ITC claim will be flagged, and a notice will inevitably follow.

Delays or failures in filing returns can also attract notices. If you fail to file returns for <Verify>three consecutive tax periods</Verify> or <Verify>six continuous months</Verify> depending on your registration type, a notice for cancellation of registration may be initiated under <Verify>Section 29</Verify>. You might also find helpful information about this process in our article about [GST registration rejected reasons](/blog/gst-registration-rejected-reasons).

Furthermore, the e-way bill system is closely monitored. Discrepancies between the value of goods transported with e-way bills and the sales declared in your returns will certainly draw the taxman's attention. E-invoicing mismatches and differences in the turnover reported in Income Tax Returns versus GST returns are other emerging areas of scrutiny.

## What are the very first three things to do when a GST notice arrives?

When a notice lands in your inbox or is delivered via the GST portal, taking immediate, structured action is crucial. Here are the first three critical steps.

### Step 1: Note the deadline and section code meticulously

The clock starts ticking the moment a notice is issued. The very first action is to identify the exact date by which you must submit your reply. This deadline is almost always explicitly stated in the notice. Standard reply periods range from <Verify>7 days</Verify> to <Verify>30 days</Verify>, depending on the urgency and the section under which it is issued. For instance, a show cause notice (SCN) under <Verify>Section 73</Verify> typically grants <Verify>30 days</Verify> for a response. Missing this deadline can lead to ex-parte orders, where the tax officer decides the case against you without hearing your side, potentially imposing severe penalties. 

Equally important is identifying the section of the Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act under which the notice is issued. The section code dictates the nature of the allegation, the potential penalties, and your specific legal defenses. A notice under <Verify>Section 61</Verify> is a mere scrutiny of returns, while one under <Verify>Section 74</Verify> implies serious allegations of fraud or suppression of facts, carrying a penalty that can be up to <Verify>100%</Verify> of the tax amount evaded. 

### Step 2: Validate the authenticity and details of the notice

Before hitting the panic button or drafting a response, you must verify that the notice is genuine and legally valid. The government has mandated that all communications from the tax department must carry a Document Identification Number (DIN). If a physical notice lacks a DIN, it is legally invalid, and you are generally not obligated to respond to it. You can verify the DIN on the official CBIC portal.

Additionally, verify the financial year and tax period mentioned. Ensure that the notice is not time-barred. The time limits for issuing an SCN under <Verify>Section 73</Verify> for non-fraud cases is <Verify>three years</Verify> from the due date of the annual return, while for fraud cases under <Verify>Section 74</Verify>, it extends to <Verify>five years</Verify>. If the notice pertains to a period beyond these limits, it can be challenged on jurisdictional grounds.

### Step 3: Collate all relevant documentation and data

You cannot build a defense on mere assertions; you need hard facts and documents. If the notice pertains to an ITC mismatch, immediately pull up your purchase registers, GSTR-2A/2B reconciliations, valid tax invoices, e-way bills, and proof of payment made to the suppliers within the mandatory <Verify>180 days</Verify>. 

If the query is about outward supplies, gather your sales registers, bank statements, and shipping documents. Organizing this data chronologically and systematically is the foundation of a strong reply. Engage your internal finance team or external consultants to conduct a thorough reconciliation of the disputed amounts. Do not submit a reply containing sweeping statements without the supporting documentary evidence attached.

## How do you draft an effective response to a GST notice?

Drafting the response requires a delicate balance of factual accuracy, legal grounding, and respectful communication. The goal is to provide a clear, unambiguous answer that resolves the officer's doubts.

Start by clearly stating the reference number, date, and subject of the notice. Address the officer respectfully, using proper designations. In the opening paragraph, explicitly state whether you agree or disagree with the contentions raised in the notice.

If you agree with the tax liability, you must pay the demanded tax along with the applicable interest under <Verify>Section 50</Verify> (currently <Verify>18% per annum</Verify>) and any penalty. For example, if you pay the tax and interest before the issuance of an SCN under <Verify>Section 73</Verify>, no penalty is levied. If paid within <Verify>30 days</Verify> of the SCN, no penalty applies. If you choose to pay, provide the payment challan details (DRC-03) in your reply to request closure of the proceedings.

If you disagree, address each point raised in the notice sequentially. Use a tabular format for clarity, comparing the department's allegations against your factual submissions. State the facts clearly. For example, "The difference in outward supply of Rs. 50,000 is due to a credit note issued on [Date], which was inadvertently omitted in GSTR-1 but considered in GSTR-3B."

Support every factual statement with a reference to the attached documents. Crucially, invoke the relevant provisions of the law, recent circulars, or judicial precedents that support your stance. The law is dynamic, and referencing a recent High Court ruling or a clarification issued by the CBIC can significantly strengthen your case. Ensure the reply is signed by the authorized signatory and filed on the GST portal within the stipulated time.

## What are the different types of GST notices and forms?

GST notices come in various forms, each serving a distinct legal purpose. Familiarizing yourself with these forms can demystify the process.

Here is a structured list of common GST notices and their implications:

| Notice Form | Section of CGST Act | Purpose and Trigger | Typical Action Required |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ASMT-10** | <Verify>Section 61</Verify> | Scrutiny of returns. Issued when the system detects mismatches (e.g., GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B, or GSTR-3B vs GSTR-2B). | Reply via Form **ASMT-11** within <Verify>30 days</Verify>, providing explanations or accepting the liability and paying via DRC-03. |
| **DRC-01** | <Verify>Sections 73 & 74</Verify> | Show Cause Notice (SCN) demanding tax, interest, and penalty. It follows ASMT-10 if the reply is unsatisfactory, or is issued directly based on audits or investigations. | Submit a detailed legal and factual reply in **DRC-06** within <Verify>30 days</Verify>. |
| **DRC-01A** | <Verify>Rule 142(1A)</Verify> | Intimation of tax ascertained before issuing an SCN. It offers a chance to pay the tax and interest to avoid an SCN and penalty. | Pay the amount via DRC-03 or file submissions in Part B of DRC-01A if you disagree. |
| **REG-03** | <Verify>Section 29</Verify> | Notice seeking clarification for new registration or proposing cancellation of existing registration (e.g., due to non-filing of returns). | Reply within <Verify>7 working days</Verify> providing the requested information or rectifying the default. |
| **GSTR-3A** | <Verify>Section 46</Verify> | Notice for not filing GST returns by the due date. | File the pending return within <Verify>15 days</Verify> of receiving the notice to avoid assessment orders. |

## What happens if the GST officer rejects your reply?

Despite your best efforts and a well-drafted reply, the proper officer may not be convinced by your submissions. If this happens, the officer will proceed to issue a formal assessment order or an adjudication order.

This order, typically issued in Form **DRC-07**, quantifies the final demand for tax, interest, and penalty. The issuance of this order creates a legally binding liability on you. The order will detail the officer's findings, the reasons for rejecting your submissions, and the exact amount you are required to pay.

Upon receiving the DRC-07 order, you have a critical decision to make. You must either pay the demanded amount or challenge the order. Ignoring the order is the worst possible course of action. If you fail to pay or appeal within <Verify>three months</Verify> (the standard appeal period), the department will initiate rigorous recovery proceedings.

These recovery mechanisms are sweeping and severe. The tax department has the authority under <Verify>Section 79</Verify> to attach your bank accounts, freeze your assets, garnish your receivables from third parties (directing your debtors to pay the government instead of you), and even seize and sell your movable or immovable property. Therefore, taking immediate action upon receiving an adverse order is non-negotiable.

## How do you challenge an adverse GST order?

If you believe the adjudication order is legally flawed, factually incorrect, or arbitrary, the law provides a robust mechanism to seek redressal. You have the right to file an appeal before the First Appellate Authority (Joint Commissioner or Commissioner of Appeals).

To file an appeal, you must act within the statutory time limit, which is strictly <Verify>three months</Verify> from the date the order was communicated to you. The appeal must be filed electronically in Form **APL-01**. For more details on the timelines and procedural nuances of appealing to higher authorities, you may read our comprehensive guide on [filing appeal to CIT(A), timelines, and fees](/blog/filing-appeal-cit-a-timelines-fees).

A crucial condition for filing an appeal is the mandatory pre-deposit. To ensure that only serious appeals are entertained and to safeguard a portion of the revenue, the law requires you to pay a percentage of the disputed tax before your appeal can be heard. Under <Verify>Section 107</Verify>, you must pay the entirety of the admitted tax liability (the portion you agree with) and a pre-deposit equal to <Verify>10%</Verify> of the disputed tax amount arising from the order. This pre-deposit is capped at a maximum of <Verify>Rs. 25 Crores</Verify> under CGST (meaning Rs. 50 Crores in total for CGST + SGST).

Once the pre-deposit is paid via the electronic cash ledger or credit ledger, the recovery proceedings for the remaining balance of the disputed amount are automatically stayed. The Appellate Authority will then grant you an opportunity for a personal hearing to present your case before passing the appellate order.

## What happens during the personal hearing?

The personal hearing is your day in court, albeit an administrative one. Whether you are responding to an initial Show Cause Notice or appearing before the Appellate Authority, a personal hearing is a critical step in the adjudication process. The principles of natural justice mandate that no adverse order should be passed against a taxpayer without giving them a fair opportunity to be heard.

During the hearing, you or your authorized representative (such as a Chartered Accountant, Cost Accountant, Company Secretary, or Legal Practitioner) can present your arguments directly to the presiding officer. This is the moment to articulate the nuances of your business, explain complex transactions that cannot be easily conveyed on paper, and emphasize the legal precedents that support your case.

It is highly recommended to prepare a 'Written Submission' or 'Paper Book' summarizing your key arguments and annexing copies of relevant case laws, circulars, and documents. Submit this during the hearing to ensure your verbal arguments become a formal part of the record. The officer will record the minutes of the hearing. Ensure that these minutes accurately reflect the core points you raised, as this forms the basis of the final order. ## Can a GST notice lead to criminal prosecution?

While most GST disputes are civil matters concerning the payment of tax, interest, and penalties, certain severe violations can attract criminal prosecution. The GST law contains stringent provisions to penalize deliberate tax evasion and fraudulent activities.

Prosecution can be launched under <Verify>Section 132</Verify> of the CGST Act. The offenses that trigger prosecution include supplying goods or services without issuing an invoice to evade tax, issuing invoices without actual supply of goods or services (commonly known as fake invoicing or bill trading), fraudulently availing ITC based on such fake invoices, or collecting tax but failing to deposit it with the government beyond a period of <Verify>three months</Verify>.

The severity of the punishment depends on the quantum of tax evaded. If the amount of tax evaded, ITC wrongly availed, or refund wrongly obtained exceeds <Verify>Rs. 500 Lakhs (Rs. 5 Crores)</Verify>, the offense is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to <Verify>five years</Verify> and a fine. For amounts between <Verify>Rs. 200 Lakhs</Verify> and <Verify>Rs. 500 Lakhs</Verify>, the imprisonment can extend to <Verify>three years</Verify>. Offenses involving amounts exceeding <Verify>Rs. 5 Crores</Verify> are non-bailable and cognizable, meaning the authorities can arrest the accused without a warrant. Given these severe implications, any notice alleging fraud or fake invoicing must be handled with extreme caution and immediate legal counsel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**1. Can I ignore a GST notice if I think it is completely wrong or auto-generated by mistake?**
No, you must never ignore any communication from the tax department, even if you believe it to be factually incorrect or absurd. Failure to reply within the stipulated time allows the officer to pass an ex-parte order, imposing a tax liability and penalty against you without hearing your side. Always file a formal reply refuting the allegations with evidence.

**2. What happens if I miss the 30-day deadline to reply to a Show Cause Notice?**
If you miss the deadline, the proper officer may proceed to adjudicate the matter based on the materials available to them. This usually results in an adverse order confirming the entire demand proposed in the notice. While you can sometimes request an extension before the deadline expires, ignoring the deadline severely weakens your position.

**3. Is it mandatory to hire a lawyer or CA to reply to a GST notice?**
Legally, it is not mandatory; you can draft and file the reply yourself. However, GST laws are highly complex and constantly evolving. Given the potential financial risks, penalties, and the legal nuances involved in interpreting statutes and case laws, it is highly recommended to seek professional assistance to ensure your response is legally sound and comprehensive.

**4. If I pay the tax demanded in ASMT-10, do I still have to pay a penalty?**
Usually, if you accept the discrepancy pointed out in ASMT-10 (Scrutiny of Returns) and pay the tax along with applicable interest under <Verify>Section 50</Verify> before any Show Cause Notice is issued, no penalty is levied under <Verify>Section 73</Verify>. However, this must be done promptly and properly intimated to the department.

**5. How long does the department have to issue a notice for a past financial year?**
For cases not involving fraud or willful misstatement (under <Verify>Section 73</Verify>), the notice must be issued at least <Verify>three months</Verify> prior to the time limit for passing the order, which is <Verify>three years</Verify> from the due date of furnishing the annual return for that financial year. For cases involving fraud (under <Verify>Section 74</Verify>), the time limit for the order is <Verify>five years</Verify>, and the notice must be issued at least <Verify>six months</Verify> prior to that.
